\title[When Does Graph Structure Help Noisy-Label IDS?]{When Does Graph Structure Help Noisy-Label Intrusion Detection?
A Leakage-Audited Study of Rare-Class Training-Signal Retention}

\abstract{Machine-learning intrusion detection is trained on imperfect labels,
and standard noisy-label pipelines delete suspicious samples---often the
rare-class rows that carry most of the learnable signal. We report a
leakage-audited boundary study of when graph structure genuinely helps
noisy-label network intrusion detection. Starting from a graph extension of
collaborative label denoising (CoLD), we froze an audited protocol---
label-construction proxy fields masked, strongest-available group-disjoint
splits, pre-registered hypothesis families with Holm correction---and
re-evaluated everything inside it on CICIDS-2017, UNSW-NB15, and the
CESNET-TLS-Year22 service-classification stress test. Three findings survive.
First (robust; 9/9 corrected tests): within a shared pipeline, soft retention
improves tail-class F1 over hard deletion ($+0.21$ to $+0.31$ on CICIDS-2017;
$+0.03$ to $+0.05$ elsewhere) at no detectable aggregate cost, and the
direction survives real labelling errors and a 100k-window scale check.
Second (null; reported with effect ceilings): a multi-view label-space graph
diagnostic adds no detectable increment over Confident Learning (18/18 tests;
Tail-F1 ceiling $+0.026$), and the specific rescue weighting adds nothing over
a simple frequency floor. Third, a four-factor leakage decomposition attributes
25--36 points of Tail-F1 inflation to duplicates, cross-split entities, proxy
fields, and clean-label oracle paths---factors that flatter deletion-style
baselines and compress the apparent advantage of retention. The audit also
yields dataset-level findings: CESNET's released features contain direct label
parents; UNSW-NB15's official partition ships with 38.4\% exact train--test
feature collisions; and $\sim$95\% of CICIDS-2017's rare ``Web Attack -- Brute
Force'' class are labelling artefacts. The protocol, regression tests, and
reproduction package accompany the paper.}

\keywords{intrusion detection, noisy labels, graph learning,
training-signal retention, leakage audit, rare-class robustness, negative results}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Machine-learning classifiers underpin modern network intrusion-detection systems
(IDS) and security operations centers (SOCs). These models are trained from labels
that are seldom perfect: they may be delayed, produced by rules of uneven quality,
or shaped by analyst workflows that emphasize high-severity cases~\cite{arp2022dodont,song2022survey}.
The resulting label noise degrades both detection quality and operational triage.
Crucially, the cost of label noise is asymmetric. Discarding a mislabeled benign
flow may raise a benchmark score, but discarding an informative low-frequency
sample can remove much of the training signal available for a rare class.

This asymmetry motivates a reframing of the problem. Conventional noisy-label
pipelines treat suspicious samples as contamination to be purged; we instead
treat data curation as a \emph{training-signal retention} problem, in which the
decision to delete weighs estimated label unreliability against the class-level
cost of losing scarce training examples. This is a mechanism-level learning
claim: benchmark rows are not, by themselves, SOC investigation or forensic
evidence. The question is therefore not only \emph{how accurately} noise can be
detected, but \emph{what should be done} with samples the detector cannot
confidently classify: delete, trust, or retain at reduced influence.

CoLD~\cite{yang2026cold} recently framed noisy-label IDS through \emph{local
consistency}: because different traffic classes can share feature neighborhoods,
label noise induces spurious associations, and a collaborative representation can
identify and remove likely-noisy samples before training. CoLD, like most
dataset-purification methods, ultimately performs \emph{hard deletion}. Starting
from a graph-based extension of this idea, we ask three questions that turn out
to have very different answers. (RQ-A)~Does replacing hard deletion with
signal-preserving soft weighting retain rare-class training signal and improve
tail-class detection? (RQ-B)~Does a multi-view graph diagnostic, kept strictly
in label space, add measurable value over precise probabilistic pruning?
(RQ-C)~How much of the apparent benefit of graph-based denoising on standard
IDS benchmarks is real, and how much reconstructs the rules by which the
benchmark labels were generated in the first place?

Answering these questions honestly required an unusual amount of methodological
hygiene, and the audit trail became a contribution in its own right. Our first
implementations produced spectacular gains that did not survive scrutiny: a
leakage audit revealed 24\% exact-duplicate CICIDS-2017 records, clean-label
paths in score and graph construction, label-construction proxy fields in the
released features, and train--test entity overlap. We therefore froze an
audited protocol---proxy-masked features, strongest-available group-disjoint
splits, pre-registered hypothesis families with Holm correction---and re-ran
everything inside it. Under that protocol the answers are: soft retention
robustly preserves tail signal relative to deletion (RQ-A, positive); the graph
diagnostic adds no detectable increment over Confident Learning, and the
specific weighting form adds nothing over a frequency floor (RQ-B, null, with
informative effect ceilings); and a four-factor decomposition attributes 25--36
points of Tail-F1 inflation to leakage factors that flatter deletion-style
methods most (RQ-C).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
The empirical record supports the following bounded contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \emph{four-factor leakage audit protocol} for noisy-label IDS
(duplicates, cross-split entities, label-construction proxy fields, clean-label
oracle paths), instantiated as frozen splits, masking tiers, and regression
tests. It quantifies 25--36 points of Tail-F1 inflation, shows that leakage
flatters deletion-style baselines and compresses the apparent advantage of
retention, and surfaces three dataset-level findings: CESNET's released
\texttt{CATEGORY} columns are direct label parents, UNSW's official partition
ships with 38.4\% exact feature collisions, and CICIDS's flow topology makes
entity-disjoint splitting infeasible by construction
(Section~\ref{sec:res-leak}).
\item A \emph{mechanism-level finding} (pre-registered family A, robust on 3/3
datasets): within a shared pipeline, soft retention improves Tail-F1 over hard
deletion under high asymmetric noise, with no detectable aggregate cost and
consistently positive macro means; the direction survives real labelling
errors and a 100k-window scale check (Section~\ref{sec:res-tail}).
\item \emph{Boundary results reported as first-class findings}: the graph
diagnostic adds no detectable increment over Confident Learning (18/18
Holm-corrected tests null; Tail-F1 effect ceiling $+0.026$), and the
evidence-rescue weighting form adds nothing over a simple frequency floor---on
one dataset it is significantly worse (Section~\ref{sec:res-nohelp}).
\item \emph{Dataset-native corroboration}: applying published corrected labels
to CICIDS shows that $\sim$95\% of its rare ``Web Attack -- Brute Force'' class
are labelling artefacts, tying the audit directly to real, non-synthetic
labelling errors (Section~\ref{sec:res-leak}).
\end{enumerate}
We deliberately bound our claims. The graph-based method is not an accuracy
advance: under the frozen protocol Confident Learning is the bar, and we make
no claim of reproducing or outperforming official CoLD (our comparator is a
CoLD-aligned proxy; Section~\ref{sec:cold-fidelity}). The distinctive value of
this study is the audit protocol, the retention mechanism, and the boundary
conditions---not a state-of-the-art number.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Noisy-label learning.}
Approaches divide into robust-training and dataset-purification families. Robust
training modifies losses or training dynamics: loss correction with a transition
matrix~\cite{patrini2017making}, peer-network small-loss selection
(Co-teaching~\cite{han2018coteaching}, Co-teaching+~\cite{yu2019coteachingplus}),
disagreement-based updates (Decoupling~\cite{malach2017decoupling}), and
semi-supervised treatment of suspected-noisy data
(DivideMix~\cite{li2020dividemix}). Purification detects and removes mislabeled
instances: confident learning~\cite{northcutt2021confident}, representation-based
filtering (FINE~\cite{kim2021fine}), and, in security, MORSE~\cite{wu2023morse}
and MCRe~\cite{yuan2023mcre}. CoLD~\cite{yang2026cold} is the closest prior work;
we evaluate against a \emph{CoLD-aligned proxy} and are explicit about what it
does and does not implement (Section~\ref{sec:cold-fidelity}). Our departure is twofold: we keep the diagnostic in label space
(avoiding the distance-metric fragility CoLD itself criticizes), and we replace
deletion with signal-preserving weighting motivated by the semi-supervised
insight that suspected-noisy samples carry usable signal~\cite{li2020dividemix,wu2023morse}.

\paragraph{Benchmark label errors and dataset audits.}
A parallel literature documents that the benchmarks themselves are flawed.
Engelen et al.~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting} diagnosed labelling and
generation errors in CICIDS-2017; Liu et al.~\cite{liu2022error} reverse-engineered
and corrected its labelling logic; Lanvin et al.~\cite{lanvin2023errors} showed
that such errors materially change measured detector performance. We build on
this line in two ways: we use the published corrected labels as a source of
\emph{real} labelling errors for validation, and we extend the error taxonomy
from label mistakes to \emph{label-construction proxy leakage}---features and
split structures that let a model reconstruct the labelling rules---with an
isolation protocol and regression tests that other studies can reuse.

\paragraph{Graph learning for security telemetry.}
Security events are relational, and graph neural
networks~\cite{kipf2017gcn,velickovic2018gat,hamilton2017graphsage,hu2020hgt}
naturally propagate contextual signals. Provenance-graph IDS such as
Flash~\cite{rehman2024flash} and Argus~\cite{xu2024argus} demonstrate the value of
structure for enterprise detection. We use graph representation learning as a
backbone but, unlike embedding-distance denoisers, keep the noise diagnostic in
label space so it stays aligned with the classifier.

\paragraph{SOC alert handling and concept drift.}
Operational systems must also cope with alert volume and distribution shift.
Alert-classification and triage work~\cite{vaarandi2024nids,chavali2024rlalert}
and drift-aware detection~\cite{yang2021cade} motivate our operational framing,
though our contribution is denoising for rare-class signal retention rather than a
deployed triage study.

\paragraph{Contrastive self-supervision.}
Our representation stage uses cross-view contrastive
learning~\cite{chen2020simclr}, adapted so that the same node in two views forms a
positive pair.

\subsection{Positioning and Open Challenges}

\subsubsection*{Noisy-label learning for intrusion detection}

Learning from noisy labels has become an important challenge in security
analytics because network telemetry is often collected through heterogeneous
sources, automated rules, and delayed analyst feedback. Unlike conventional
computer vision benchmarks where label corruption can often be controlled
synthetically, security labels may reflect incomplete attack knowledge,
imbalanced detection rules, and evolving adversarial behaviors
\cite{guerra2022datasets,qing2023lowquality}. Therefore, improving robustness
requires not only higher classification accuracy but also understanding which
samples should be trusted and which samples contain useful but uncertain training signal.

Existing noisy-label learning methods can be broadly divided into robust
training and dataset purification. Robust training approaches attempt to reduce
the influence of corrupted samples during optimization. Loss correction methods
estimate label transition characteristics and modify the training objective
\cite{zhao2021enhancing}. Sample-selection approaches exploit uncertainty,
loss dynamics, or disagreement between models to identify potentially noisy
instances \cite{xia2021sample,liu2021metric}. Co-teaching and related
disagreement-based approaches train multiple networks and exchange selected
small-loss samples to reduce memorization of corrupted labels
\cite{han2018coteaching,yu2019coteachingplus}. Decoupling further separates the
decision of when to update from how to update, demonstrating that disagreement
between classifiers can provide useful information under label corruption
\cite{malach2017decoupling}.

A different family focuses on dataset purification. Confident Learning estimates
label uncertainty by identifying statistically inconsistent annotations
\cite{northcutt2021confident}. FINE explores representation-space filtering of
noisy instances through latent feature structures
\cite{kim2021fine}. In security domains, MCRe and MORSE extend noisy-label
handling toward malicious traffic and malware classification scenarios
\cite{yuan2023mcre,wu2023morse}. More recently, intrusion detection studies
have explored contrastive learning and representation enhancement as a way to
improve robustness against noisy or incomplete observations
\cite{wang2022feco,yue2022cleid}.

CoLD provides the closest foundation for our work by introducing collaborative
label denoising specifically for network intrusion detection. However, existing
purification-oriented methods generally treat suspicious samples as removable
noise. This assumption is problematic for imbalanced classification because
rare-class samples may simultaneously appear inconsistent and be important to
the learned decision boundary.
Graph-CoLD therefore does not aim to replace purification, but instead explores
whether structural context and signal-preserving weighting can retain
informative suspicious instances.

\subsubsection*{Graph learning and contrastive representation for security}

Graph representation learning has become increasingly important for security
analytics because network activities naturally form relational structures among
hosts, addresses, sessions, and temporal events. Graph convolutional networks
and graph attention networks demonstrated that neighborhood information can
improve representation learning when samples are connected through meaningful
relations \cite{kipf2017gcn,velickovic2018gat}. Inductive graph representation
learning further enables scalable modeling of previously unseen nodes
\cite{hamilton2017graphsage}, while heterogeneous graph transformers extend
graph reasoning to multiple relation types \cite{hu2020hgt}.

In security applications, graph methods have mainly focused on attack
detection, provenance analysis, and contextual behavior modeling. Systems such
as Flash demonstrate the value of provenance graph representation learning for
enterprise intrusion detection \cite{rehman2024flash}. Other work investigates
security-oriented representation learning and graph analytics for detecting
suspicious behaviors \cite{xu2024argus}. These approaches usually focus on
discovering malicious structures from graph embeddings.

Graph-CoLD differs from these methods in its objective. The graph structure is
not directly used as an anomaly detector; instead, it provides contextual
signals for a label-space consistency diagnostic. This distinction is
important because embedding distance alone may capture feature similarity while
remaining disconnected from the classifier's final prediction behavior. By
combining graph context with prediction-space disagreement, Graph-CoLD aims to
identify unreliable labels while preserving samples that remain informative.

\subsubsection*{SOC alert triage, prioritization, and concept drift}

Modern SOC environments face not only detection accuracy challenges but also
large-scale alert management problems. Excessive alerts, evolving attack
behaviors, and changing network distributions create operational pressure for
analysts. Alert classification and active-learning approaches have explored
ways to reduce analyst workload by improving alert categorization
\cite{vaarandi2024nids}. Reinforcement-learning-based alert prioritization
methods further investigate how automated systems can rank alerts according to
operational importance \cite{chavali2024rlalert}.

Beyond static classification, concept drift has become a critical challenge in
long-running security monitoring. Detection systems must adapt when normal
traffic patterns, attack strategies, and service behaviors evolve. Studies such
as CADE, RecDA, and Insomnia investigate drift detection and adaptation
mechanisms for network intrusion detection
\cite{yang2021cade,yang2024recda,andresini2021insomnia}. Recent SOC-oriented
research also explores alert aggregation and filtering through deep learning
models to improve investigation efficiency
\cite{liu2025deepdrac,shon2023semisup,wang2024alertagg}.

These operational studies motivate our security framing, but Graph-CoLD
addresses a different layer of the problem. Rather than replacing analyst
triage or predicting attack priority directly, we focus on improving the
quality of the training data that supports downstream detection. The key
difference is that Graph-CoLD combines three principles: label-space
consistency diagnosis, signal-preserving retention, and leakage-audited
evaluation. This design aims to answer when graph-based denoising genuinely
helps security analytics and when structural information is insufficient.
\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}
Let $V$ be the set of training flows and $\mathcal{M}$ the set of \emph{active}
graph views. Each view $m\in\mathcal{M}$ induces a graph $G^{m}=(V,E^{m})$ over the
same nodes. The observed training label $\tilde{y}_v$ may differ from the
unobserved clean label $y_v$. We seek a classifier that (i) resists the effect of
noisy labels, and (ii) retains useful training signal---especially clean samples
of rare classes that a purifier is tempted to discard.

We adopt three design principles. First, the noise diagnostic operates in
\emph{label/prediction space}, not embedding distance, so it remains aligned with
the classifier and does not re-introduce the local-consistency fragility of
distance-based purifiers. Second, denoising is \emph{signal preserving}: a
suspicious but informative sample is down-weighted rather than removed. Third, the
evaluation is traceable to audited real data, with explicit scope limits.

\label{sec:method}

representation learning, followed by a label-space consistency diagnostic that
drives a signal-preserving weight (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

\subsection{Multi-view graph construction}
Views are built from semantically related feature blocks over the training nodes:
a \emph{host} view links samples sharing endpoint identifiers; an \emph{IP} view
uses communication/flow statistics, ports, protocol and TLS metadata; a
\emph{temporal} view links samples within a time window; a \emph{behavioral} view
groups service, protocol, port-state, and related behavioral feature blocks
(it is a feature-block view, not host-process lineage) and is used only where such
fields exist. Support is part of the reproducible
dataset contract: CICIDS-2017 activates host/IP/temporal, CESNET-TLS-Year22
activates IP/temporal, and UNSW-NB15 activates temporal/behavioral. Threat-intelligence
views are disabled when the fields are absent. Crucially, edges never cross the
train/test split, and (Section~\ref{sec:res-leak}) exact/near-duplicate edges are
removed.

\subsection{Self-supervised representation}
Each active view produces a node embedding $z_v^{m}$ from Bernoulli-masked input
features, $x'_v = x_v \odot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(1-\delta)$. Cross-view contrastive
learning treats the same node in two views as a positive pair and other batch
nodes as negatives:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}} = -\log
\frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z^{a}_v,z^{b}_v)/\tau)}
{\sum_{u}\exp(\mathrm{sim}(z_v,z_u)/\tau)} .
\end{equation}
Temporal alignment and reconstruction terms regularize the embedding,
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{rep}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\text{temporal}}
+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}$; active views are fused by mean aggregation to
obtain $z_v$.

\subsection{Graph-CDM: a label-space consistency diagnostic}
For each node $v$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{GraphCDM}(v)=\lambda_1 D_{\text{pred}}(v)+\lambda_2 D_{\text{neigh}}(v)
+\lambda_3 D_{\text{view}}(v),
\end{equation}
where the prediction term compares per-view predicted labels with the observed
\emph{noisy} training label,
\begin{equation}
D_{\text{pred}}(v)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}[\tilde{y}^{m}_v\neq \tilde{y}_v],
\end{equation}
the neighborhood term is a label-space KL divergence between a node's soft
prediction and its neighborhood mean,
$D_{\text{neigh}}(v)=\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\hat{y}_v \,\|\,
|N(v)|^{-1}\sum_{u\in N(v)}\hat{y}_u\big)$,
and the view term measures active-view mode disagreement,
$D_{\text{view}}(v)=1-\max_c\frac1M\sum_m\mathbf{1}[\tilde{y}^{m}_v=c]$.
A fourth provenance-chain term explored in early drafts is removed (frozen
$\lambda_4{=}0$): none of the three datasets carries verified provenance chains,
and no reported result depends on it. No term uses clean labels or the noise
mask; this is enforced by a regression test.

\subsection{Signal-preserving soft weighting}
The internal evidence score protects samples that would be costly to discard,
$e(v)=\text{freq\_protect}(\tilde{y}_v)\,(1+\gamma\,\text{anom}(v))$, min-max
normalized to $\tilde{e}(v)$. Rather than a small additive floor (which collapses
to hard deletion), we use an evidence-\emph{rescue} form so that suspicious yet
high-evidence samples retain real weight:
\begin{equation}
w(v)=\underbrace{\sigma\!\big(-\kappa(\mathrm{GraphCDM}(v)-\theta)\big)}_{\text{clean branch}}
+\underbrace{\big(1-\sigma(\cdot)\big)\,\lambda_{r}\,\tilde{e}(v)}_{\text{evidence rescue}} .
\end{equation}
The classifier minimizes $\sum_v w(v)\,\mathrm{CE}(f(z_v),\tilde{y}_v)$. Setting
$\lambda_r=0$ and thresholding recovers hard deletion; this is our
\texttt{ablation\_hard} comparator, sharing the identical graph, representation,
and diagnostic so that the ablation isolates \emph{only} the weighting.
Two further frozen comparators isolate whether the specific \emph{rescue form}
(rather than retention per se) carries the benefit: a frequency-floor
reweighting $w(v)=\max(\tau_{\text{ret}},\,\mathrm{freq}(v))$ with
$\mathrm{freq}(v)=\min(1,\,\mathrm{median\ class\ count}/\mathrm{count}(\tilde{y}_v))$,
and a no-graph class-balanced cross entropy with effective-number weights
($w_c\propto(1-\beta)/(1-\beta^{n_c})$, $\beta{=}0.9999$)~\cite{cui2019classbalanced};
both share the encoder and training budget (pre-registered family~C,
Section~\ref{sec:res-tail}).

We stress the intended interpretation of this internal score: ``evidence'' here
means a heuristic estimate of training-signal retention value, not SOC or
forensic evidence. Frequency protection encodes \emph{deletion cost}, not
trustworthiness. A rare sample is protected because discarding it is costly---it
may be one of few training examples for a tail class---not because rarity implies
a clean label.
The score is thus a heuristic proxy for a risk-aware retention signal
$R_{\text{delete}}(v)\propto \mathrm{Cost}(\tilde{y}_v)\,\big(1+\gamma\,\text{anom}(v)\big)$,
where $\mathrm{Cost}$ is high for rare classes, and the weighting in
Eq.~(4) implements risk-aware sample retention: samples with high diagnostic
suspicion but high deletion cost keep reduced---never zero---influence.
A full decision-theoretic treatment that models
$P(\text{clean}\mid x)\cdot\mathrm{Cost}(y)$ explicitly is left to future work;
the empirical ordering in Section~\ref{sec:res-tail}, where
confident-learning-precision pruning outperforms our heuristic, indicates the
value of sharpening the $P(\text{clean}\mid x)$ component.
A structural limitation is that $\text{freq\_protect}$ is keyed on the
\emph{observed} noisy label $\tilde{y}_v$: under asymmetric tail-to-majority
noise, a flipped tail sample is observed as the majority class and receives
weaker protection, while a majority sample mislabelled into a tail class is
over-protected. Consistent with this, the pre-registered family~C comparison
(Section~\ref{sec:res-tail}) finds no detectable advantage of the rescue form
over the simple frequency floor---retention itself, not the weighting form,
carries the benefit.
Both the CoLD-aligned proxy baseline and the \texttt{ablation\_hard} variant use the
same shared encoder architecture, training budget, and view-masking configuration
as Graph-CoLD; they differ only in the presence or absence of graph views and the
weighting rule applied after Graph-CDM scoring.

\subsection{Graph-CoLD Optimization Procedure}

The complete Graph-CoLD pipeline consists of representation learning,
label-space consistency estimation, retention scoring, and weighted classifier
optimization. Algorithm~\ref{alg:graphcold} summarizes the complete procedure.

\begin{center}
\refstepcounter{algorithm}\label{alg:graphcold}
\begin{minipage}{0.92\linewidth}
\textbf{Algorithm~\thealgorithm: Graph-CoLD signal-preserving noisy-label learning.}

\textit{Input:} Multi-view graphs $\{G_m\}_{m=1}^{M}$, noisy labels
$\tilde{Y}$, feature matrix $X$. \\
\textit{Output:} Trained classifier $f$.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Construct active graph views according to available telemetry fields.
\item Learn view-specific representations using contrastive, temporal-alignment,
and reconstruction objectives, then fuse them into node embeddings $Z$.
\item Generate prediction-space and neighborhood consistency statistics and
compute a Graph-CDM score for each training sample.
\item Estimate internal retention scores from label frequency and anomaly
factors, then compute the signal-preserving weights $w(v)$.
\item Optimize the classifier using weighted cross entropy and return $f$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

The design follows three considerations. First, the diagnostic operates in label
and prediction space rather than pure embedding distance. This maintains
consistency with the classifier decision process and avoids relying solely on
representation similarity. Second, the weighting mechanism adopts an
signal-rescue formulation because suspicious samples may still contain useful
class information. A simple lower bound would approximate hard deletion,
whereas the rescue formulation allows uncertain but informative samples to
participate in training. Third, hard deletion is retained as the primary
ablation because it isolates the contribution of signal preservation while
keeping the same graph representation and diagnostic components.

\subsection{Frozen configuration and tuning discipline}
\label{sec:frozen-config}
All experiments use one frozen configuration, fixed before the final experiment
grid and archived with the reproduction package: diagnostic weights
$\lambda_{\text{pred}}{=}0.4$, $\lambda_{\text{neigh}}{=}0.3$,
$\lambda_{\text{view}}{=}0.3$ ($\lambda_4{=}0$, removed); weighting
$\kappa{=}4.0$, $\theta{=}0.5$, $\lambda_r{=}0.8$, $\gamma{=}1.0$,
log-scaled $\text{freq\_protect}$, retention floor $\tau_{\text{ret}}{=}0.1$;
hard-deletion threshold $\mathrm{CDM}\le0.5$ on the shared diagnostic.
Graph construction: host/IP views use $k{=}10$ Euclidean nearest neighbours,
the temporal view links $k{=}2$ neighbours within a 3{,}600\,s window; the
encoder is a 2-layer, 4-head heterogeneous graph transformer (hidden 128,
dropout 0.2), trained 100 epochs (stage 1) plus 50 (stage 2), learning rate
$10^{-3}$, batch 128. \emph{Tuning discipline:} no clean validation set and no
true injected noise rate is available to any method at any point; hyperparameter
selection uses defaults or criteria computed on the noisy training set only
(e.g.\ cleanlab-estimated noise rates for the Co-Teaching forget schedule).
This discipline is part of the frozen protocol and applies identically to every
comparator.
\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:exp}
\subsection{Datasets and protocol}
Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes three benchmark datasets with different tasks.
CICIDS-2017 and UNSW-NB15 are intrusion-detection datasets. CESNET-TLS-Year22~\cite{hynek2024zenodo}
is a TLS service-classification dataset whose SNI-derived application labels do
not carry attack/benign semantics; we retain it only as a long-tail service-class
stress test and do not use it to support attack-level claims. CICIDS-2017 is used under
an 11-class \emph{post-filter} policy with exact deduplication (24\% of raw
training rows removed; Section~\ref{sec:res-leak}). CESNET-TLS-Year22 is a
year-spanning backbone TLS dataset used under a 25-service-class audit-window policy (it
replaces MALTLS-22, for which we lacked a verified license path). UNSW-NB15 is
used under its partition with the smallest class (Worms) removed. Noise
semantics per dataset: the asymmetric flip target is benign for the two IDS
datasets and a high-frequency service class for CESNET; legacy result filenames
may use \texttt{tail-to-benign} as an implementation label, but that term has no
semantic meaning for CESNET.

All headline results use a \emph{frozen final protocol}, fixed before the final
experiment grid; any later deviation is disclosed as a protocol amendment
(Appendix). The protocol has two ingredients per dataset. (i)~\emph{Proxy-field
masking}: features that participate in each dataset's label construction, or are
strong proxies of it, are removed before any graph or model sees the data
(CICIDS: tier-1 mask; CESNET and UNSW: tier-2, which additionally removes the
\texttt{CATEGORY} one-hot columns---parents of the CESNET label---and the
\texttt{ct\_*} generated features of UNSW). (ii)~\emph{Strongest-available
group-disjoint split}: CICIDS uses a temporal split ordered by source-file day
(with exact training-row deduplication); CESNET uses a strict temporal boundary
with zero train--test entity overlap (S1$\cap$S2); UNSW uses its official fixed
partition, whose released files lack timestamp and entity columns (a limitation
we measure rather than hide: the official partition itself contains 38.4\%
exact train--test feature collisions after masking).

\paragraph{Split taxonomy and audit measurements.}
The audit distinguishes S0 (iid stratified), S1 (temporal), S2 (entity-disjoint,
grouped so that no entity appears on both sides), and S3
(leave-one-scenario-out, defined only where the scenario is not identical to the
class label: attack-day holdout for CICIDS; undefined for UNSW and CESNET, where
S1$\cap$S2 substitutes). For every split we measure and report the train--test
exact and near-duplicate intersection (cosine similarity $\ge 0.999$ on
standardized features). Test-time inference is inductive (per-sample); a
transductive variant that builds graphs over test batches is reported only as a
control and does not represent an online IDS. On CICIDS, entity isolation is
infeasible by construction---the flow graph collapses into one giant connected
component spanning 99.96\% of valid flows---which we document as a dataset-level
finding (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Noise injection.}
Noise is injected into training labels only; test labels stay clean. The
rare-class analysis uses asymmetric noise concentrated on tail classes (training
class count $\le$ window median) at rates $\{40,60,80\}\%$ with seeds $0$--$9$
bound to the injection. Flip targets are the benign class for the two IDS
datasets and the high-frequency service class \texttt{microsoft-diagnostic} for
CESNET, which has no benign semantics. Symmetric and graph-consistency noise
were explored only under the legacy protocol and are not part of the formal
result matrix. The complete mapping matrix ships with the reproduction
package (\texttt{noise\_map.json}).

\paragraph{Pre-registered statistical protocol.}
The inference unit is the per-noise-rate paired seed comparison ($n{=}10$); we do
not pool the 30 seed--rate observations as independent. Holm correction is
applied within three pre-registered families: family~A (9 tests; soft retention
vs.\ hard deletion, Tail-F1), family~B (18 tests; CL{+}Graph-CDM vs.\ CL,
Macro- and Tail-F1---the G2 decision gate), and family~C (18 tests; soft weight
vs.\ simple reweighting baselines, Tail-F1). ``No aggregate cost'' claims are
replaced by TOST non-inferiority at $\delta{=}0.01$ Macro-F1; because a
$0.01$ macro change could hide a concentrated single-class regression, a
per-class guard additionally requires no class-level F1 regression above
$0.05$. Seed random-intercept mixed-effects models provide robustness
confirmation. Success requires Holm $p<0.05$, $d_z\ge0.3$, and a bootstrap 95\%
CI excluding zero; the verdict vocabulary is frozen
(\emph{robust}: $\ge$2 datasets pass; \emph{narrow}: 1; \emph{null}: 0).

\paragraph{Windows and scale.}
Each dataset uses a deterministic audit window of 10{,}000 training and 5{,}000
test instances drawn inside the frozen split. A one-shot scale validation on
CICIDS (train 100{,}000 / test 20{,}000; 3 seeds $\times$ 3 rates) checks that
conclusions do not flip with scale; they do not (Section~\ref{sec:res-tail}).
We are explicit that this is a focused audit study, not a full-archive
evaluation.

\subsection{Comparators and metrics}
Within the frozen protocol we compare six configurations that share one encoder
architecture and training budget: Graph-CoLD-soft; hard deletion
(\texttt{ablation\_hard}); the frequency-floor and class-balanced-CE
comparators of Section~\ref{sec:method}; Confident Learning
(cleanlab \texttt{find\_label\_issues} with \emph{full stratified out-of-fold}
probabilities, per its sample-out protocol, and a shared-budget classifier
head)~\cite{northcutt2021confident}; and the CL{+}Graph-CDM combination that
soft-reweights CL-retained samples (issue-set intersection/union fusions are
exploratory). The rows labelled \emph{CoLD-aligned proxy} were called ``CoLD''
in an earlier draft; the implementation predates the official CoLD code release
and does not implement its multi-subset encoding, clustering-consistency
purification, or XGBoost head. We therefore make no claim of reproducing or
outperforming official CoLD, whose published numbers arise under a different
nine-class protocol (Section~\ref{sec:cold-fidelity}). FINE~\cite{kim2021fine}
and a fully-implemented Co-Teaching~\cite{han2018coteaching} (forget schedule
driven by an \emph{estimated}, not oracle, noise rate) were run under the legacy
stratified audit window and are reported separately as explicitly
non-comparable (Section~\ref{sec:res-tail}). Metrics: aggregate Macro-F1,
tail-class Macro-F1 (tail $=$ classes with training count $\le$ window median,
fixed per data contract), tail recall, and paired deltas with bootstrap 95\%
CIs under the statistical protocol above.

\subsection{RQ-A: Soft retention preserves tail signal; the specific weighting form does not matter}
\label{sec:res-tail}
\paragraph{Family A (robust).}
Table~\ref{tab:family-a} reports the pre-registered soft-vs.-hard comparison
under the frozen protocol. All nine conditions pass: Holm-corrected $p<0.05$,
effect sizes $d_z$ from 0.77 to 3.14, and bootstrap CI lower bounds above zero
on every dataset and rate. The effect is large on CICIDS ($+0.21$ to $+0.31$
Tail-F1) and small-to-moderate on CESNET/UNSW ($+0.03$ to $+0.05$). Aggregate
cost is bounded on three sides: TOST non-inferiority at $\delta{=}0.01$ passes
9/9, the per-class guard finds no class regression beyond $-0.036$
(threshold $0.05$), and paired macro means are themselves positive
($+0.014$ to $+0.062$)---so we state ``no detectable aggregate cost, with
consistently positive macro means,'' not ``no cost.'' A seed random-intercept
mixed-effects model confirms the result on each dataset (mean $\Delta$ Tail-F1
$+0.044$/$+0.261$/$+0.039$ for CESNET/CICIDS/UNSW, one-sided $p<10^{-4}$).
Mechanistically, hard deletion removes clean rare-class training rows flagged
as suspicious; soft weighting keeps them at reduced influence.

\paragraph{The direction survives real labelling errors and scale.}
Replacing synthetic injection with the real labelling errors of CICIDS-2017
(training on original released labels, evaluating against Engelen-corrected
labels~\cite{liu2022error}; Section~\ref{sec:res-leak}) preserves the
direction: soft $-$ hard Tail-F1 $=+0.284$ $[+0.089, +0.487]$
(Table~\ref{tab:natural-noise}; descriptive, 10 paired seeds). A one-shot
scale validation at train 100{,}000 / test 20{,}000 on CICIDS also preserves
the direction at every rate ($+0.150$/$+0.056$/$+0.031$), while showing that
window size itself dominates tail learnability (hard deletion reaches 0.698
Tail-F1 at rate 0.4 in the 100k window vs.\ 0.093 in the 10k window).

\paragraph{Family C (null): the rescue form adds nothing over simple reweighting.}
Table~\ref{tab:family-c} tests whether the specific evidence-rescue form---the
design element that distinguishes our weighting from trivial baselines---beats
a frequency floor or class-balanced CE. It does not: no condition survives
Holm correction in the positive direction, and on UNSW the soft weight is
significantly \emph{worse} than the frequency floor at rates 0.4/0.6 (CI upper
bounds $-0.011$/$-0.013$). We therefore withdraw the claim that evidence-rescue
weighting is superior to simple reweighting. The surviving mechanism claim is
deliberately narrow: \emph{retention} beats \emph{deletion} (family A); among
retention schemes, the weighting form is empirically interchangeable, and a
practitioner can obtain the benefit with a frequency floor alone.

\subsection{RQ-B: When graph structure does not help}
\label{sec:res-nohelp}
This section collects the negative results as first-class findings.

\paragraph{Family B (null): no detectable increment over Confident Learning.}
The pre-registered decision gate for the graph diagnostic asked whether
soft-reweighting the samples that Confident Learning retains adds anything over
CL alone. Table~\ref{tab:family-b} reports the complete 18-test family: no
test is significantly positive after Holm correction (all $p\ge0.608$), and the
largest Tail-F1 CI upper bound is $+0.026$ (CESNET, rate 0.6)---an informative
effect ceiling, not merely an absence of evidence. Mixed-effects robustness
models agree (per-dataset mean $\Delta$ Tail-F1 $+0.004$/$-0.014$/$-0.007$,
none supporting an increment). We accordingly withdraw the claim that
Graph-CDM adds increment over precise probabilistic pruning and reposition it
as an audit/diagnostic instrument.

\input{tables_w5/tab_family_b}

\paragraph{Saturation at scale.}
In the 100k-window scale validation, CL alone is near ceiling (Tail-F1
0.95--0.995); the combination has no headroom, and the rate-0.8 point estimate
is negative ($-0.014$ $[-0.042, +0.001]$). Under real labelling errors the
ordering is the same: CL reaches 0.910 Tail-F1 vs.\ 0.864 for
Graph-CoLD-soft (Table~\ref{tab:natural-noise}).

\paragraph{Transductive construction adds nothing.}
A transductive control that builds graphs over test batches performs
\emph{worse} than the inductive classifier on all three datasets in the audit
screening (e.g.\ CICIDS 0.603 vs.\ 0.684 Macro-F1), ruling out---for this
implementation---the concern that test-batch information drives the reported
numbers. This is evidence about our pipeline, not a general claim about
transductive methods.

\paragraph{Boundary conditions as testable hypotheses.}
Where might a graph diagnostic still add value? The informativeness pattern
(Section~\ref{sec:res-info}) and the family-B ceiling suggest two registered
hypotheses for future work rather than salvage claims: (i) graphs built from
verified relational telemetry (host lineage, authenticated flows) rather than
feature-space neighbourhoods; (ii) noise processes correlated with graph
structure. Our real-noise experiment does not support the second hypothesis
for CICIDS's actual labelling errors, where CL remains ahead.

\subsection{RQ-C: Where inflated numbers come from (leakage decomposition)}
\label{sec:res-leak}
\paragraph{Historical account.}
Our initial pipeline reported a $\sim$28-point CICIDS advantage with a curve
that barely degraded under 60\% noise---implausible for a denoiser. The first
audit found $152{,}201/630{,}399$ ($\approx$24\%) exact-duplicate CICIDS
training rows and clean-label paths in score/graph construction; removing them
collapsed the apparent advantage (to $+6.7$ points on CICIDS under the interim
audit protocol). Those interim numbers motivated the frozen protocol and are
superseded by the systematic decomposition below.

\paragraph{Four-factor decomposition under the final protocol.}
Table~\ref{tab:leakage} flips one leakage factor at a time against the frozen
protocol: duplicate records (DUP), cross-split entities (ENT),
label-construction proxy fields (PRX), a clean-label oracle path in the
diagnostic (ORA), and all at once (ALL). On CESNET, entity overlap and proxy
fields contribute $+0.187$ and $+0.184$ Tail-F1 each, approximately additive
(ALL $+0.249$ soft; $+0.359$ for the deletion-style proxy)---a 25--36 point
inflation across methods and metrics, reproducing the historical ``+28-point''
figure in controlled form. On UNSW the picture inverts: the official fixed
partition already contains 38.4\% exact train--test feature collisions, so
adding deduplication \emph{lowers} scores by 0.115 (duplicates inflate the
frozen numbers from inside the official split), and unmasking \texttt{ct\_*}
proxies is net negative because feature-space collisions dilute
($-0.052$).

\input{tables_w5/tab_leakage}

\paragraph{Leakage flatters deletion, not retention.}
The decomposition reverses a natural misreading of our earlier drafts. Leakage
factors inflate \emph{deletion-style} methods most (CICIDS CoLD-aligned proxy:
DUP $+0.467$, ALL $+0.389$; hard deletion $+0.375$/$+0.296$), because under
the clean protocol they delete tail classes nearly to zero (proxy Tail-F1
0.000 on CICIDS) and any leakage ``finds back'' those classes for free. Soft
retention, having already banked its tail gains, has little headroom (CICIDS
DUP $+0.244$ with CI crossing zero). Consequently, leaky protocols
\emph{compress} the soft-vs.-hard gap (CICIDS: 0.256 under the frozen protocol
vs.\ 0.125 under DUP)---they flatter deletion baselines and mask their true
tail cost. The oracle path (ORA) likewise helps only deletion-style methods.

\paragraph{Dataset-level findings.}
Three findings are properties of the benchmarks themselves and transfer beyond
this study: (i) CESNET's released features include \texttt{CATEGORY} one-hot
columns that are parents of the label---a direct label proxy; (ii) UNSW's
official partition ships with 38.4\% exact feature collisions after masking;
(iii) CICIDS's flow graph collapses into one giant connected component
(99.96\% of valid flows), making entity-disjoint splitting infeasible by
construction (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Real labelling errors corroborate the audit.}
Applying the published Engelen relabelling rules~\cite{liu2022error} to the
original CICIDS release yields $128{,}247$ corrected rows (4.53\% of valid
flows): $65{,}634$ missed portscan rows and $31{,}339$ missed DDoS rows were
released as BENIGN, while $95\%$ of the ``Web Attack -- Brute Force'' class
(1{,}434 of 1{,}507 rows) are attack-orchestration artefacts, leaving only 74
true positives. Rare-attack classes in this benchmark are, to a large extent,
artefacts of the labelling pipeline---direct, dataset-native corroboration of
the label-construction-proxy concern that motivates our audit protocol
(cf.~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting,lanvin2023errors}).

\subsection{Graph informativeness (descriptive, hypothesis-generating)}
\label{sec:res-info}
A per-dataset neighborhood-homophily / view-coverage diagnostic, computed under
the legacy protocol, is directionally consistent with where graph-based
processing helped most in that era: CICIDS (rich host/IP/temporal views) showed
the largest margins, CESNET sat near a performance ceiling, and UNSW
(temporal/behavioral views only) the smallest
(Figure~\ref{fig:info}). With only three datasets this pattern is descriptive
and hypothesis-generating, not inferential, and under the frozen protocol the
family-B result shows that whatever signal the views carry does not translate
into an increment over precise pruning. We keep the diagnostic because it is
cheap to compute and, as an \emph{audit} instrument, it flags upfront when a
dataset's views are too weak for graph construction to be worth the cost.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discuss}
% <<P4-DISCUSSION>>
% BLOCK D -> <<P4-DISCUSSION>>

The operational motivation is strongest in settings where multiple telemetry
sources provide partially consistent signals, such as host relationships,
communication patterns, and temporal behavior. In those settings, a suspicious
sample need not be useless: a rare attack instance may appear inconsistent
because it represents an emerging technique rather than an incorrect label. Our
benchmark study tests only this training-data mechanism; it does not establish
deployment suitability.

However, the results also demonstrate clear boundaries. When available views
are weak or incomplete, graph structure provides limited additional information.
The UNSW-NB15 results illustrate this condition: without sufficiently
informative relational views, graph-based diagnostics cannot reliably create
additional training signal. Similarly, datasets operating near a performance ceiling,
such as CESNET-TLS-Year22 under the evaluated protocol, naturally limit the
observable improvement.

\subsection{CoLD fidelity: what the proxy is and is not}
\label{sec:cold-fidelity}
The comparator historically labelled ``CoLD'' in earlier drafts is a
\emph{CoLD-aligned proxy}: a neighbourhood consistency diagnostic with hard
thresholding and a shared classifier head, built before the official CoLD code
release. It does not implement official CoLD's multi-subset feature encoding,
clustering-consistency purification, or XGBoost pipeline. The official NDSS
paper reports Macro-F1 in the 0.94--1.00 range on a nine-class refined
CICIDS-2017 under random 8:2 splits---a protocol that differs from our
11-class, tail-targeted, group-disjoint audit windows in labelling space,
split design, and noise model, so the numbers are not comparable in either
direction. We use the proxy solely as a deletion-style anchor inside our frozen
protocol and make no claim of reproducing or outperforming official
CoLD~\cite{yang2026cold}. An exact-alignment grid under the official protocol
is registered as future work.

A key methodological decision in this work is the avoidance of unfair
state-of-the-art comparisons. Confident Learning was therefore run inside the
frozen protocol itself (Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:family-b}), even
though it outperforms the graph-based combination wherever a difference is
detectable; that result is part of the empirical record, not an inconvenience.
Other purification and robust-training
variants (MCRe, MORSE, Decoupling) were not
reimplemented under the final leakage-audited protocol, and FINE/Co-Teaching ran
only under the legacy window (Table~\ref{tab:baselines-iid}). Mixing results obtained
under different data-cleaning, duplication-removal, and evaluation settings
would create an unreliable comparison, so those methods remain excluded from
the formal matrix. A comprehensive comparison with all previous methods
requires faithful implementations or official code under a unified evaluation
framework.

The frozen-protocol ordering carries a practical lesson for benchmark data
curation and a hypothesis for later SOC-facing validation. Confident Learning's
precise pruning sets the performance bar wherever a difference is detectable,
while the retention-style reweightings form one narrow band
(Table~\ref{tab:main}). The differentiator is not deletion versus retention but
\emph{diagnostic precision}: an imprecise purifier sacrifices scarce rare-class
training signal whether it deletes samples or down-weights them insufficiently,
and family C shows the specific weighting form matters little once retention is
granted. Sharpening the $P(\text{clean}\mid x)$ component---for example by
feeding confident-learning-grade probabilities into a retention-aware
weighting---is the natural next step, and our family-B result gives the effect
ceiling such a combination must beat.

The leakage audit provides a broader methodological lesson for graph-based
security machine learning. Graph models can unintentionally exploit duplicated
records, cross-split connections, or hidden clean-label information. Such
effects may produce impressive but misleading improvements. Therefore, graph
security studies should explicitly verify data separation, graph construction
constraints, and label independence before reporting performance gains.

Future work should extend the audit protocol toward streaming SOC environments,
full-archive evaluation, analyst-in-the-loop validation, and broader
cross-domain benchmark comparisons. These extensions are necessary to determine
whether signal-preserving retention remains effective when labels arrive
continuously and attack distributions evolve over time.
The results support a deliberately narrow claim set. Within a shared pipeline,
soft retention preserves rare-class training signal that hard deletion
discards---robustly across datasets, noise rates, real labelling errors, and
window scale. Beyond that, our experiments do \emph{not} support: an accuracy
advantage for graph-based denoising (family B is null against Confident
Learning), a superiority of the specific rescue weighting (family C is null,
with one significant reversal), a ranking or triage advantage, or SOC/forensic
effectiveness of benchmark-level sample retention.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\emph{Internal.} The evaluation uses deterministic audit windows for
tractability; absolute scores are far below full-archive numbers, and the 100k
scale check shows window size itself dominates tail learnability. The paired,
seed-controlled family comparisons---not the absolute numbers---are the objects
of inference. Two protocol deviations occurred and are disclosed as amendments
(Appendix~\ref{app:amendments}): family B was initially run at full power on
only one dataset (remediated by complete reruns), and the soft-retention claim
was initially supported only by screening-tier evidence (remediated by the
full family-A grid).
\emph{Construct.} Injected noise is synthetic; we mitigate this with the
real-labelling-error experiment, which preserves the family-A direction. The
legacy rare-evidence-recovery diagnostic was tautological and is excluded from
all hypothesis families. Tail-class definitions are window-relative and frozen
per data contract.
\emph{External.} CICIDS-2017, CESNET-TLS-Year22, and UNSW-NB15 are benchmarks,
not SOC deployments; no analyst study is claimed. The strongest available
split differs per dataset (UNSW's official partition lacks timestamp/entity
columns and carries 38.4\% exact collisions we measure but cannot remove
without leaving the official split). MCRe and MORSE pass a clean-label sanity
floor but were not re-run under the frozen protocol; we report caveats rather
than possibly unfaithful numbers.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
Five limitations bound the scope. (i)~\emph{CICIDS entity isolation is
infeasible by construction}: the flow graph over the raw release forms one
giant connected component covering 99.96\% of valid flows, so the cross-split
entity factor cannot be measured on this dataset; we document the topology
rather than substitute a weaker entity definition. (ii)~\emph{UNSW's official
partition} carries 38.4\% exact train--test feature collisions after masking;
we keep the official split for comparability and measure the collision effect
in the leakage decomposition instead of silently repartitioning.
(iii)~\emph{CESNET supports only service-class claims}: its SNI-derived labels
have no attack semantics, so attack-level conclusions are limited to
CICIDS/UNSW. (iv)~\emph{The CoLD-aligned proxy is not official CoLD}; no
cross-comparison with published CoLD numbers is valid in either direction, and
an official-code alignment grid is registered as future work.
(v)~\emph{TOST non-inferiority at $\delta{=}0.01$} bounds only the aggregate:
the margin equals a worst-case single-class F1 change of $\sim$0.11 in an
11-class task, which is why the per-class guard accompanies every
non-inferiority claim. MALTLS-22 and OpTC are not evaluated (no verified
license/provenance path); behavioral and threat-intelligence views are
disabled where fields are absent; full-archive and streaming evaluations are
future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We set out to extend collaborative label denoising with graph structure and
ended up with a boundary study. Under a frozen, leakage-audited protocol---
proxy-masked features, group-disjoint splits, pre-registered hypothesis
families---the surviving positive result is mechanism-level and robust: soft
retention preserves rare-class training signal that hard deletion discards,
across three datasets, three noise rates, real labelling errors, and a
100k-window scale check, at no detectable aggregate cost. The graph diagnostic
itself adds no detectable increment over Confident Learning (effect ceiling
$+0.026$ Tail-F1), and the specific rescue weighting adds nothing over a
frequency floor---results we report as first-class findings with effect
ceilings rather than as absences. The four-factor leakage decomposition
explains where the earlier, larger numbers came from: duplicates, cross-split
entities, proxy fields, and oracle paths inflate deletion-style baselines most
and compress the apparent advantage of retention. More broadly, our results
argue that security data curation should shift from indiscriminate purification
toward signal-aware retention, in which deletion decisions explicitly weigh
the class-level cost of discarding scarce training examples against the
precision of the noise diagnostic---and that graph-based denoising results in
security should be accompanied by a leakage audit before any performance claim
is made. CESNET supports only a service-class stress-test claim; attack-level
conclusions are limited to CICIDS and UNSW. We hope the audit protocol, the
regression tests, and the honestly reported boundaries are as useful to the
community as any single number.

\section{Protocol amendments}
\label{app:amendments}
The protocol was frozen before the final experiment grid. Two deviations
occurred; both are disclosed here in full, with their remediations.

\paragraph{PA-1: selective family-B testing (remediated).}
Family B was frozen as 3 datasets $\times$ 2 metrics $\times$ 3 noise rates
$=$ 18 tests. In the initial full-power run, only CESNET was executed at full
power; CICIDS and UNSW were stopped after a screening pass (3 seeds, one rate)

pre-registered and constituted selective testing. As remediation, the
full-power protocol (10 seeds $\times$ 3 rates, each dataset's frozen primary
protocol) was executed on the two remaining datasets and the Holm correction
recomputed over the complete 18-test family with no placeholders
(Table~\ref{tab:family-b}). The screening-stop decision is disclosed as a
post-hoc choice; the G2 verdict (null) is unchanged under the complete family.

\paragraph{PA-2: claim strength ahead of evidence (remediated).}
The soft-retention finding was initially reported as a core claim while
supported only by screening-tier evidence (3 seeds, one rate). The frozen
protocol requires full inference for headline claims. As remediation, the
complete family-A grid (10 seeds $\times$ 3 rates $\times$ 3 datasets under the
